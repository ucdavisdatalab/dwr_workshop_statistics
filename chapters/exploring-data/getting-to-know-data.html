1  Getting to Know the Data

NoteLearning Goals

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain what it means for a dataset to be “tabular”
  • Explain what a “feature” is
  • Explain what an “observation” is
  • Explain what it means for a dataset to be “tidy”
  • Describe some widely-used data types
  • Convert columns to appropriate data types

This chapter explains where to start when you explore a dataset. Whether it’s data you collected yourself or a new and unfamiliar dataset, the steps to go through and details to check at the beginning of an exploration are the same. Some of these are qualitative: now is a good time to think about how and why the data were collected, and whether they’re appropriately representative to address the questions you want to answer. Others are computational, such as whether the dataset’s structure matches expectations and is convenient for analysis. The chapter also briefly discusses fixes for common problems, with references to where you can learn more.

1.1 Be a Data Biographer

The first step in getting to know a dataset is to understand why and how it was collected. This will help you identify:

  • Who or what the dataset represents;
  • What kinds of questions you can use it to answer;
  • What kinds of analysis methods might be appropriate;
  • What biases or limitations it might have.

We recommend taking some time to write a brief description of the dataset in your own words, called a data biography. Do this even if the dataset’s documentation already includes a description or biography, to help you organize and clarify your understanding of the dataset.

Use the documentation and related publications as you write. If the dataset doesn’t have documentation or the documentation is incomplete, you might need to do some sleuthing with the dataset itself (see the remainder of this reader), online, or by contacting the people who created the dataset directly. Keep track of any unresolved questions about the dataset as you write the biography.

A data biography serves as a reference as you analyze the dataset and communicate results. If you collected the dataset yourself and plan to share it with others, the data biography is also essential documentation for helping the audience make sense of the dataset. Even if you didn’t collect the dataset, the biography and notes you write during analysis might be helpful to colleagues or collaborators who also want to work with the dataset.

The following subsections break down some of the kinds of questions to ask as you write a data biography.

Important

Keep the biography questions in mind throughout your time working with the dataset. When you arrive at results you want to share, revisit the questions before doing so.

1.1.1 Context and Motivation

First, think about where the dataset came from—its provenance. Ask:

These questions can help you identify potential biases or limitations of the dataset. Consider whether the person or group who collected the dataset has any conflicts of interest. While conflicts of interest are not necessarily indicative of a problem with a dataset, they do suggest it deserves extra scrutiny for biases.

As an example, consider a dataset collected by a light bulb manufacturer about the operating characteristics of their bulbs. Most likely, their motivation is to convince potential customers that their bulbs are superior to competitors, although it might also be to assess how they can improve their bulbs. In other words, they have an obvious conflict of interest. As a result, they might use a testing process that doesn’t replicate typical household conditions, selectively include results from their tests, or aggregate the results in a way that makes the bulbs appear to have especially long lifespans or low power consumption.

When thinking about what a dataset represents, data scientists (and statisticians) usually think in terms of the population from which the data were drawn. The population might consist of people, but it could also consist of other animals, plants, inanimate objects, or something else. The members of the population are called subjects or sometimes units of observation. Some datasets represent or are associated with multiple populations.

1.1.2 Collection Strategy

Next, think about how the dataset was collected. Ask:

The question about population gets at whether the dataset is a sample: a subset of subjects selected from a population. When it’s not possible to collect data about an entire population—whether due to cost, difficulty, ethical concerns, or something else—we might instead take a sample. The hope is that the sample is representative, meaning results for the sample generalize to the entire population.

How well a sample represents a population depends on how it was collected and how large it is, among other things. A simple random sample, where subjects are selected from the population randomly and independently, often generalizes well because random sampling preserves most statistical properties of the population. In experiments, researchers sometimes use more sophisticated sampling procedures to control for various biases.

As you think about the population and sample procedure, it’s especially important to consider whether there are subgroups within the population that might be underrepresented or left out entirely, whether by coincidence, due to natural constraints, or intentionally. These can limit the generalizability of results from the dataset.

1.1.3 Precautions and Impact

Finally, think about how using the dataset might affect other people or things. Ask:

These questions are intended to help you reflect on potential ethical and legal concerns when working with the dataset, as well as your own responsibilities to the associated population(s) and sampled subjects.

The CARE Principles (Carroll et al. 2020), which were originally developed to protect Indigenous peoples’ rights and interests with respect to Indigenous data, provide a basis for reasoning about the impacts of data work on communities more generally. The principles are:

  • Collective benefit: data and analyses should provide benefit to the community as a whole and reflect community values.
  • Authority to control: community members have a right to and should be actively involved in the stewardship and governance of their data.
  • Responsibility: data workers have a responsibility to engage and cultivate respectful relationships with community for the benefit of the community.
  • Ethics: protecting the rights and well-being of community members should be the highest priority; community members must be allowed to assess benefits and harms themselves.

The CARE Principles have been adopted by organizations such as the Research Data Alliance and the Smithsonian Institution.

NoteSee Also

The Turing Way (2025), an online textbook developed by data scientists and other researchers from all over the world, incorporates a wide variety of perspectives about best practices for collecting, analying, and communicating results about data. This chapter discusses the CARE Principles.

Data Feminism (D’Ignazio and Klein 2020) is a textbook that explores how to approach data work in an ethical and equitable way by drawing from feminist principles. The publisher provides a free, online version of the book in addition to selling print and eBook versions.

1.2 Structure Matters

The shape and organization of a dataset, together called structure, govern how you can access and query its data. Depending on the structure, some analyses are easier to carry out than others. Most datasets can be stored in and transformed between a variety of structures, each with different advantages and disadvantages.

When you start working with a dataset, identify the structure and think about whether it’s suitable for the analyses you want to carry out. If it isn’t, transform to a more suitable structure. The focus of this reader is exploration, so we won’t cover how to transform datasets.

NoteSee Also

If you want to learn more about how to transform a dataset with R, see DataLab’s Intermediate R: Cleaning and Reshaping Data workshop reader.

A tabular dataset is one that’s structured as a table, with rows and columns. This reader focuses on tabular datasets, since they’re common in practice and present the fewest programming challenges. Here’s an example of a tabular dataset:

Fruit Quantity Price
apple 32 1.49
banana 541 0.79
pear 10 1.99

Some other examples of datasets and structures are:

  • People in a family, such as the Roosevelts, might be organized as a (family) tree, with connections from parents to children.
  • Monthly population estimates for a fish species in the Sacramento River might be a series of numbers.
  • Relationships between cats in a small town might be a graph (or network), with a node for each cat and an edge (connection) between each pair of cats that have met.
  • Bike paths in Davis, CA might be a collection of geospatial coordinates that describe points, lines, and shapes.
  • Individual consumer reviews for several different vacuums might be a table with a row for each review and columns to identify the individual, vacuum model, and review score.

1.2.1 Features and Observations

Data scientists think about datasets in terms of two components:

  • A feature (also called a covariate) is measurement of something, usually across multiple subjects. For example, we might decide to measure the heights of everyone in the class. Each person in the class is a subject, and the height measurement is a feature. Features don’t have to be quantitative. If we also asked each person their favorite color, then favorite color would be another feature in our dataset. In tabular datasets, features are usually, but not always, the columns.

  • An observation is a set of features measured for a single subject or at a single time. So in the preceding example, the combined height and favorite color measurement for one student is one observation. In tabular datasets, observations are usually, but not always, the rows.

Every dataset has features and observations, regardless of whether the dataset is tabular.

Note

In statistics, features are often called variables, because they can be modeled as values that vary randomly. This idea is different from variables in programming languages (like R), which are just names for values.

For clarity, in this reader we only use the word “variable” in the programming language sense. We always call measurements in a dataset “features” or “covariates.”

A data dictionary is documentation that describes each feature in a dataset in detail. For a tabular dataset, a data dictionary typically describes the unit of observation (in other words, what a subject is) and what each column measures. The description explain the range of realistic values, regardless of whether the feature is quantitative or qualitative. For quantitative features, it should also specify the units of measurement.

We’ll see an example of a data dictionary in Section 1.3.

1.3 Dataset: CA Least Terns

The California least tern is a endangered subspecies of seabird that nests along the coast of California and Mexico. The California Department of Fish and Wildlife (CDFW) monitors least tern nesting sites across the state to estimate breeding pairs, fledglings, and predator activity in each annual breeding season.

The CDFW publishes most of the data it collects to the California Open Data portal. The examples in this and subsequent chapters use a cleaned 2000-2023 version of the California least terns dataset.

1.3.1 Writing a Data Biography

To get started with the California least terns dataset, let’s try to answer the data biography questions from Section 1.1. We’ll go through them one-by-one and use the source page as reference documentation.

Who collected the dataset?

The dataset was assembled by the CDFW. The documentation says “data is reported to CDFW” but doesn’t say by whom. Possibly the data are collected by members of CDFW, members of other government agencies, or volunteers.

Why did they collect the dataset?

The documentation doesn’t explain why CDFW collects the dataset, but CDFW’s website says that California least terns are an endangered species with state and federal protections. Thus it is likely CDFW collects the dataset to monitor the population of the birds and advise policymakers.

What do they claim the dataset represents?

According to the documentation, the dataset consists of annual census data for California least tern nesting sites, including estimated counts of fledglings, breeding pairs, and nests.

What was the collection procedure?

The documentation is notably vague on this point. If you wanted to use the dataset for research purposes, it would be prudent to contact CDFW to request more information about this.

When and where did they collect the dataset?

The dataset spans spans all of California and covers multiple years (the source page doesn’t say which years, but we established earlier that the cleaned DataLab version covers 2000-2023).

Does the dataset consist of the entire population? If not, how were subjects selected?

Observations are at the year-site level. It’s possible that there are some nesting sites for which data were not collected. The documentation doesn’t explain how nesting sites were located and whether some might have been missed. Again, this is something we could contact CDFW about to get more details.

Who is impacted by use or analysis of the dataset? What are the potential consequences?

Results from analyzing this dataset could benefit or harm California least terns. For instance, they could be used to argue for policies that change the level of protection for the species. This could have far-reaching implications for the ecology of California and surrounding regions.

Are any special privacy or security precautions necessary?

The CDFW masked the exact geospatial coordinates of the nesting sites because this is a public dataset and the birds are endangered. In other words, CDFW already addressed the primary concern.

1.4 Structural Summaries

After reading a dataset into R (or other statistical software), examining its structure is one of the first things you should do. This way you can identify and correct potential problems, and prepare the dataset for the analyses you’d like to carry out.

R provides several functions for getting structural summaries of data. Some of these are:

  • str to print a compact structural summary
  • head, tail to get the first or last few elements
  • nrow, ncol, dim, length to get dimension information
  • names, colnames, rownames to get element names
  • class to get classes (R data types; see Section 1.5)

We’ve already seen the head function, but let’s try a few more of these on the California least terns dataset (Section 1.3).

1.5 Data Types

It’s useful to categorize data into types based on characteristics they have in common, such as what they are and what we can do with them. For example, if the data are numbers, we can summarize them by computing a sum or an average. We can’t use these summaries if the data are categories, such as genres of music (pop, blues, classical, etc.).

In other words, types tell us about which methods are possible and potentially appropriate. They’re a convenient way to reason about how to transform, analyze, and present data. That said, keep in mind that they’re just a hint—you still need to use your own knowledge and judgement, too!

Statisticans conventionally think in terms of these data types:

  • Categorical, which includes:
    • Nominal: data separated into specific categories, with no order. For example, hair color (red, brown, blonde, …) is nominal.
    • Ordinal: data separated into specific categories, with an order. For example, school level (elementary, middle, high, college) is ordinal.
  • Numerical, which includes:
    • Discrete: integers or specific decimal numbers with no values in between. Sometimes discrete values can also be treated as ordinal. For example, month as a number (1, 2, …, 12) is discrete.
    • Continuous: decimal numbers. There are no specific categories, but there is an order. For example, height in inches is continuous.

We can and will often think and talk about types of features rather than types of individual values, because each feature measures one thing. For instance, a feature that measures temperature in degrees Celsius will have numeric (and probably continuous) values. So we say the feature is numeric.

When each feature is in its own column, it also makes sense to talk about types of columns. R assumes this is true in the str function’s structural summary, which shows a type for each column, as well as in the output of a few other functions.

1.5.1 R Classes

Most programming languages also categorize data into types. R actually uses two separate systems for categorizing data objects:

  1. The class of an R object describes what the object does or the role that it plays. Some objects can do more than one thing, so objects can have more than one class. The class function returns the classes of its argument.

  2. The type of an R object describes how the object is stored in computer memory. Each object has exactly one type. The typeof function returns the type of its argument.

When we’re exploring a dataset with R, classes are far more important than types. If you aren’t sure what an object is, checking its classes should be the first thing you do.

The built-in classes most relevant to exploring datasets are:

Class Example Description
logical TRUE, FALSE Logical (or Boolean) values
integer -1L, 1L, 2L Integer numbers
numeric -2.1, 7, 34.2 Decimal (real) numbers
complex 3-2i, -8+0i Complex numbers
character "hi", "YAY" Text (strings)
factor Treatment A, Treatment B Categories

These are just a few of the many classes in R.

Important

Classes say what we can do with an object in R rather than what we can do statistically.

It’s up to you to think about what your dataset represents, decide which questions you want to answer, identify the statistical data types, and decide which methods are appropriate. Programming languages (and even artificial intelligence) can’t reliably do this for you.

Let’s use the class function to check the classes of a few of the columns in the California least terns dataset. First, we’ll look at the year column:

class(terns$year)
[1] "integer"

The class is integer, which makes sense since years are integer numbers. This means R will allow us make comparisons, do arithmetic, and do other math with the year column. Integers are a kind of discrete data. We could also think of year as categorical: for instance, to group the observations by year. In most cases, R is flexible enough to let us use discrete data, like integers, as categorical data.

Now let’s look at the site_name column:

class(terns$site_name)
[1] "character"

The class is character, because the site names are text. As we explore the dataset, we’re more likely to use them as categories for filtering and grouping observations than we are to use them as text to (for instance) change the capitalization of or count the number of letters in the names. So we should convert the column to the factor class, both to clarify our intent and because it will be more convenient for some of the functions we might want to use.

Important

CSV files don’t distinguish between textual and categorical data. When you read one with read.csv, recent versions of R just assume anything that might be text is text. So after reading a CSV file, you should always check whether any of the columns need to be converted to factors.

Technically, CSV files don’t record data types at all. R is usually able to guess appropriate types for non-text data, but it’s still wise to check the types of all of the columns.

Several other file formats, like fixed width (FWF) and tab-separated value (TSV) files, also have this weakness.

For the California least terns dataset, it looks like the site_name, region_3, and event columns all contain categories but have class character. Section 1.5.2 explains how to convert them to factors.

1.5.2 Fixing Problems

We can convert a column into a factor with R’s factor function. Let’s try it for the site_name, region_3, and event columns in the California least terns dataset:

terns$site_name = factor(terns$site_name)
terns$region_3 = factor(terns$region_3)
terns$event = factor(terns$event)
NoteSee Also

For more about factors, see the Factors section of DataLab’s R Basics workshop reader.

Tip

There’s another way we could’ve done this that uses only two lines of code, no matter how many columns there are:

cols = c("site_name", "region_3", "event")
terns[cols] = lapply(terns[cols], factor)

You can learn more about the lapply function in the The purrr Package section of DataLab’s R Basics workshop reader.

You can use whichever approach is more convenient and makes more sense to you. If there were other columns to convert, we’d go through the same steps with the appropriate conversion function.

R provides also as. functions to convert to the most common data types. For instance, as.character converts an object to a string:

x = 3.1
class(x)
[1] "numeric"
y = as.character(x)
y
[1] "3.1"
class(y)
[1] "character"

The read.csv function does a good job at identifying columns of numbers, so it’s rarely necessary to convert columns of numbers manually. However, you may have to do this for data you got some other way (rather than loading a file). For instance, it’s common to make these conversions when scraping data from the web.

It’s also a good idea to convert categorical columns into factors with the factor function and to convert columns of dates into dates.
